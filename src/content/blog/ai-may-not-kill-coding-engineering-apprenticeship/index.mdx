Every experienced software engineer has a story about the first time they broke something.

Perhaps it was a program that refused to compile. A database query that returned the wrong results. A production bug that took hours to understand. Or a seemingly simple feature that became complicated the moment it touched an existing codebase.

Those experiences were not wasted time.

They were how engineers learned.

You started by writing small functions. You fixed bugs. You read other people's code. You struggled with unfamiliar frameworks, learned how APIs behaved, and gradually understood why software that looks correct can still fail in production.

Nobody begins their career knowing how to design reliable systems.

Engineering expertise develops through practice, mistakes, feedback and responsibility.

But something fundamental is changing.

AI can now explore repositories, write functions, implement features, investigate errors, generate tests and modify multiple files. More advanced coding agents can execute commands, interact with development environments and work through multi-step engineering tasks.

According to GitHub's October 2026 Octoverse report, more than 40% of pull requests on GitHub began with an agent's first draft in September 2026, compared with 17% in March.

The way software gets produced is changing rapidly.

Companies see opportunities to build more with smaller teams. Experienced developers can delegate repetitive implementation work. People without traditional programming backgrounds can build applications that previously required dedicated engineering support.

But there is a question we should be asking alongside all this excitement:

**If AI starts performing the work through which people traditionally become experienced engineers, where will tomorrow's senior engineers come from?**

I believe this is one of the most important and least discussed questions in the AI revolution.

And to understand why, we need to go back to a time when the word *computer* described a human occupation.

## 1. The First Computers Were People. They Even Had Job Titles.

In 1915, a young mathematician named Sallie E. Pero appeared in the New York City census with an unusual occupation.

Her job was listed as *computer*.

She wasn't operating an electronic machine. She was a human being employed to perform mathematical calculations.

Pero had graduated from Barnard College in 1913, received its mathematics prize and completed graduate studies at Columbia University. She subsequently joined American Telephone and Telegraph (AT&T) as a human computer.

Her story is documented by the Smithsonian American Women's History Museum in its article, [Before Computers Were Machines, They Were Women](https://womenshistory.si.edu/blog/computers-were-machines-they-were-women-here-are-six-places-where-human-computers-built-modern).

It is a fascinating reminder of how much the meaning of a profession can change.

For centuries, mathematical calculations were performed by people. They worked on astronomy, navigation, engineering, military research, telecommunications and scientific problems.

These jobs required mathematical ability, accuracy and considerable patience.

As demand for scientific and industrial calculations increased, human computing became an established occupation.

Then electronic computers arrived.

The first electronic computing machines began entering practical use in the decade following the Second World War. Their development accelerated as governments and scientific organisations required increasingly complex calculations.

But people did not disappear from computing immediately.

The Smithsonian documents that human computation remained common until the 1980s, when electronic computing capacity finally caught up with demand.

Think about the transformation.

Machines gradually took over much of the repetitive calculation. But computation itself became more important than ever.

The discipline expanded into scientific computing, programming, computational mathematics, software engineering and digital infrastructure.

The occupation changed. The underlying field grew.

There is another important detail in this history.

Women played a substantial role in human computing, but their participation in computing occupations declined for approximately two decades after peaking in the late 1980s.

Technological advancement did not guarantee that everyone working in the previous occupation would benefit equally from the transition.

**Technology can create entirely new professional opportunities while simultaneously closing familiar routes into employment.**

That distinction is worth remembering when discussing AI and software development.

The disappearance of a particular task does not necessarily mean the disappearance of the knowledge behind it.

But it also does not guarantee that the people performing that task will automatically find a better opportunity.

## 2. Programmers Have Been Automating Their Own Jobs for More Than 70 Years

There is an interesting irony in the history of software development.

Programmers have repeatedly created technologies that automate work programmers previously performed.

During the 1940s and early 1950s, programming was closely connected to the hardware on which a program would execute.

Machine instructions and assembly languages required developers to understand processor instructions, memory limitations and hardware-specific behaviour.

Producing software demanded considerable manual effort.

Then came a series of innovations that changed the profession.

In 1952, mathematician Grace Hopper completed A-0, an early compiler-like system that allowed computer users to work with English-like words rather than directly entering numerical instructions.

A few years later, IBM's FORTRAN project demonstrated the potential of higher-level programming languages.

Instead of manually expressing every operation using low-level machine instructions, developers could represent mathematical and computational operations in a language designed for human understanding.

There was scepticism about whether compilers could produce sufficiently efficient programs.

But FORTRAN demonstrated that higher-level programming and compilation could work.

As John Backus documented in his historical paper, [The History of FORTRAN I, II, and III](https://research.ibm.com/publications/the-history-of-fortran-i-ii-and-iii), the development of these technologies fundamentally changed how programmers worked.

And the transformation continued.

| Period | Development | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1940s–1950s | Machine code and assembly | Developers worked close to hardware instructions |
| 1950s–1960s | Compilers and higher-level languages | Developers described operations at a higher level |
| 1960s–1980s | Structured programming | Programs became easier to organise and maintain |
| 1980s–2000s | IDEs and code generation | More repetitive development activities were automated |
| 2000s–2010s | Frameworks and libraries | Developers reused existing implementations |
| 2010s–2020s | Cloud and managed infrastructure | Infrastructure became increasingly abstracted |
| 2022–2026 | Generative AI and coding agents | Machines increasingly participate in reasoning, implementation and verification |

Look at what happened.

Developers stopped writing every machine instruction manually.

They adopted compilers.

They adopted libraries and frameworks.

They adopted managed infrastructure.

Each generation of tools reduced the amount of low-level work required to create software.

But there is an important difference between those transitions and today's AI revolution.

A compiler translates instructions. A framework provides reusable functionality. A cloud platform abstracts infrastructure.

An AI agent can increasingly interpret an objective, investigate an existing repository, propose a solution, implement changes and execute tests.

**Earlier abstractions automated parts of programming. AI is increasingly attempting to automate entire sequences of engineering work.**

That is a considerably more significant change.

It deserves more serious attention than simply calling AI another autocomplete tool.

## 3. Remember When Spreadsheets Changed the Meaning of Accounting?

Software engineering is not the only profession that has experienced technological disruption.

Consider accounting.

Before personal computers became widely available, accounting departments relied on manual records, calculators, ledgers and repetitive financial calculations.

A single change in one figure could require recalculating several other values.

Financial modelling was time-consuming.

Then, in 1979, Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston introduced VisiCalc for the Apple II.

VisiCalc automated the recalculation of spreadsheet values. Users could change a number and immediately see the implications across related calculations.

The Computer History Museum describes VisiCalc as the application that helped turn personal computers into business machines.

[Computer History Museum — VisiCalc and the personal computer revolution](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/personal-computers/17/305).

The spreadsheet transformed how people worked with financial information.

It reduced the effort required for repetitive calculations and made financial modelling more accessible.

But financial expertise did not become unnecessary.

Understanding financial statements, evaluating business performance, forecasting cash flows and making investment decisions remained important.

The technology changed the economics of performing particular tasks.

And that leads to an important question for software engineering.

What happens when the repetitive work through which people learn a profession becomes automated?

Consider a junior developer's traditional responsibilities:

- Implementing straightforward features.
- Writing CRUD operations and simple APIs.
- Fixing defects.
- Creating unit tests.
- Updating documentation.
- Investigating unexpected application behaviour.

These activities may look routine to an experienced engineer.

But they are also educational.

They teach developers how real applications behave, how systems interact, how errors emerge and why apparently simple changes can introduce unexpected consequences.

Imagine AI agents performing a substantial proportion of this work.

A company might discover that experienced engineers can supervise implementation that previously required a much larger team.

The company becomes more productive.

But what happens to people who would previously have been hired to perform those entry-level activities?

This is where the spreadsheet analogy becomes particularly interesting.

**A profession can survive technological change while its traditional career pathway becomes weaker.**

That may be a much bigger challenge than the automation of individual programming tasks.

## 4. Automation Creates Winners and Displaced Workers. It Does Not Guarantee Everyone a Better Job.

There is an economic framework that helps explain why technological progress can produce such different outcomes.

Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo explored this in their 2019 paper, *Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor*, published in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*.

Their framework describes two important effects.

### The displacement effect

Automation allows machines to perform tasks previously undertaken by human workers.

Businesses can produce the same output using fewer human hours.

For example, an organisation that previously needed ten people to perform repetitive data-processing work might need significantly fewer after introducing automation.

Productivity increases.

But labour demand for those particular tasks may decline.

### The reinstatement effect

Technological change can also create new tasks in which human labour has a comparative advantage.

New products, services, industries and responsibilities can create opportunities for workers.

Electronic computing created demand for programming and computational expertise.

Spreadsheets enabled new forms of financial modelling and analysis.

Cloud computing created entire categories of infrastructure, security and platform engineering work.

These developments created new economic activities.

But there is something people often overlook:

**The reinstatement effect is not guaranteed to compensate for displacement.**

New occupations may emerge slowly. They may require different skills. They may be located in different markets. Workers who lose their existing jobs may not automatically qualify for the opportunities that follow.

The research is available through the [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.33.2.3).

Now apply the framework to AI.

Imagine a company that previously required twenty developers to build and maintain its software products.

With increasingly capable coding agents, perhaps five experienced engineers can supervise work that previously required a much larger implementation team.

The company can produce more software.

But the other fifteen developers do not automatically move into newly created occupations.

For that to happen, the economy must generate additional demand for products, services and tasks that require people.

The decisive question becomes:

Will AI create enough new economic activity to offset the human work it automates?

History gives us examples of both displacement and new opportunities.

It does not give us a guarantee.

## 5. AI Is No Longer Just Suggesting Code. It Is Changing How Software Gets Built.

To understand the scale of the transition, we need to examine what AI coding systems can actually do.

Earlier coding assistants primarily suggested code snippets, completed lines and helped explain programming errors.

Modern agentic development environments can operate across larger portions of the development workflow.

They can:

- Explore unfamiliar repositories.
- Read project documentation.
- Identify relevant files and dependencies.
- Plan implementation changes.
- Modify multiple files.
- Execute terminal commands.
- Generate and run tests.
- Investigate failures.
- Work with specialised tools and other agents.

Products such as Claude Code and GitHub Copilot illustrate this movement towards repository-level and task-oriented development.

The important difference is that developers are increasingly delegating tasks rather than requesting individual lines of code.

And this change is already visible in industry data.

According to GitHub's [Octoverse 2026 report](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/):

- More than 40% of pull requests began with an agent's first draft in September 2026.
- The comparable figure was 17% in March.
- Agents started approximately 110 million pull requests in September alone.

These numbers do not mean that agents independently designed, validated and delivered every change.

Human review, integration and approval still matter.

But they demonstrate that AI agents are becoming a significant part of software production.

The developer's role is changing from directly performing every implementation step towards specifying objectives, coordinating tools, examining results and deciding whether the system is correct.

That is not the end of engineering.

It is a change in the distribution of engineering work.

## 6. The Employment Numbers Tell a More Complicated Story Than Either Side Admits

One side says software engineering is finished.

The other says AI will create so much software that there will be more developer jobs than ever.

Both arguments are premature.

Let's examine the evidence.

### Stanford: The warning sign is appearing among younger workers.

In August 2026, researchers Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen updated their Stanford Digital Economy Lab study, *Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence*.

Using ADP payroll data covering millions of US workers through June 2026, the researchers found no evidence of widespread economy-wide job displacement.

However, they identified a concerning difference.

Employment among workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations stood approximately 19% below where it would have been if it had kept pace with employment among similarly aged workers in less-exposed occupations.

Experienced workers showed no comparable gap.

The authors also found that the divergence appeared primarily through reduced hiring rather than increased separations.

This distinction is extremely important.

The figure does not mean that software developer employment declined by 19%.

It applies to a broader group of AI-exposed occupations and measures a relative employment gap against a comparison-based trajectory.

It is an early warning, not proof that AI alone caused the difference.

[Stanford Digital Economy Lab — August 2026 research](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/).

### Indeed: Software developer vacancies are recovering, but the growth is concentrated.

Indeed Hiring Lab published another interesting analysis in July 2026.

Its US data showed that software development job postings had increased by almost 15% since February 2025, while overall job postings had declined by approximately 7%.

But the composition of that recovery deserves attention.

Between May 2025 and May 2026:

- Senior roles accounted for 71% of the increase in software development postings.
- Jobs mentioning AI in their titles accounted for 37%.
- These categories overlap.

Even after the rebound, software development postings remained approximately 27.5% below their pre-pandemic level.

This is not evidence that software engineering is disappearing.

But neither is it evidence of an equal recovery across all experience levels.

[Indeed Hiring Lab — AI and Job Postings: From Destruction to Creation?](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/).

### Anthropic: Even an AI company is changing its junior hiring patterns.

Anthropic published an especially interesting internal employment analysis in October 2026.

The company's research found that the share of junior-level employees in its technical departments declined from 21% to 14% since early 2025.

The proportion of junior technical hires declined from 27% to 9%.

The research describes how AI agents changed the way its technical teams approached implementation and debugging work.

This is an important case study because Anthropic operates at the frontier of AI adoption.

But we need to be precise.

Anthropic is one company, not a representative sample of the entire software industry. Its employment patterns should not be treated as universal labour-market statistics.

Still, they provide an example of how AI adoption can influence organisational hiring decisions.

[Anthropic — How Employment Is Changing at Anthropic, October 16, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic).

### The productivity question is not settled either.

In July 2025, METR published a randomised controlled trial involving 16 experienced open-source developers working on 246 tasks in repositories they knew well.

The study found that participants took approximately 19% longer when using the early-2025 AI tools examined in the experiment.

That finding was surprising.

However, it would be misleading to use it as evidence that AI universally makes developers slower.

The study concerned a particular group of experienced developers, particular repositories and an earlier generation of AI tools.

In its February 2026 update, METR explained that its newer experiment suffered from participant-selection effects and unreliable time measurements for developers using multiple agents.

It therefore considered the later data an unreliable estimate of the true productivity effect.

The appropriate conclusion is that productivity depends on the task, developer, codebase, model and working process.

More generated code does not automatically mean more completed engineering work.

Sources:

- [METR — July 2025 developer productivity study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/).
- [METR — February 2026 experiment update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/).

### What about the long-term demand for software engineers?

There is also evidence against declaring the profession finished.

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects software developer employment to grow by 15.8% between 2024 and 2034, representing approximately 267,700 additional positions.

This is an official employment projection, not a promise that AI will create those jobs.

It also does not tell us how entry-level hiring will evolve.

[BLS — Artificial Intelligence, Information Technology, and Employment, 2024–2034](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/artificial-intelligence-information-technology-and-employment-2024-34.htm).

Put these findings together and a more complicated picture emerges:

Software production can increase while the number of people required per application decreases.

Total developer vacancies can recover while junior hiring remains under pressure.

Experienced developers can become more productive while new graduates struggle to acquire their first professional opportunity.

These outcomes can coexist.

And that is why the future of software engineering deserves more than a simple prediction about whether coding will disappear.

## 7. The Real Crisis May Be the Disappearance of the Engineering Apprenticeship

This is where I think the conversation becomes particularly important.

Imagine a new graduate joining a software engineering team.

Traditionally, the first few months might involve:

Reading the existing codebase.

Understanding the architecture.

Fixing relatively small defects.

Implementing simple features.

Writing tests.

Learning how the team reviews and deploys code.

Gradually taking ownership of more complex components.

The work is not glamorous.

But it introduces a developer to the realities of software engineering.

You learn that a function can pass its unit tests and still break a production workflow.

You learn that a database change can affect several services.

You learn that a seemingly harmless configuration change can create a security problem.

You learn why logging, monitoring, error handling and rollback strategies matter.

And perhaps most importantly, you learn how to investigate problems when the obvious explanation is wrong.

That is what turns someone who can write code into someone who understands systems.

Now imagine companies automating a large proportion of those early responsibilities.

Why hire several junior developers to implement routine features when an experienced engineer can delegate much of that work to AI agents?

From a short-term business perspective, this decision may make sense.

But consider the long-term consequence.

**Where will the next generation of experienced engineers gain the experience that companies increasingly expect them to have?**

This is not just a theoretical concern.

In an October 2026 discussion on r/developersIndia, a fresher asked whether there was still a point in entering software engineering when AI systems could perform work previously associated with entry-level developers.

Another widely discussed thread in r/ExperiencedDevs raised concerns about junior developers producing code with AI assistance without necessarily understanding the implementation or knowing how to investigate errors independently.

These discussions are anecdotal, not scientific evidence of industry-wide behaviour.

But they reveal a very real concern among people entering the profession and people responsible for mentoring them.

There is a difference between producing working code and understanding why that code works.

And that difference matters.

If organisations reduce entry-level opportunities without developing alternative training systems, they risk creating a bottleneck in the professional development pipeline.

Tomorrow's senior engineers need opportunities to become senior engineers.

That responsibility cannot simply be delegated to a coding agent.

## 8. The Next Engineer Will Need More Than the Ability to Generate Code

So what should engineers do?

I don't believe the answer is to abandon programming.

Nor do I believe that learning another programming language or becoming an expert prompt writer is sufficient protection against automation.

AI will continue improving across multiple technical activities.

The goal should be to develop deeper engineering capability.

I would think about the future of engineering through five interconnected abilities.

### 1. Understand systems, not just individual functions.

Learn how applications work as complete systems.

Understand databases, APIs, networks, distributed systems, authentication, operating systems and cloud infrastructure.

When AI modifies several components simultaneously, understanding their interactions becomes especially important.

### 2. Learn to investigate, not just generate.

Do not allow AI to become the only way you solve problems.

When something breaks, learn to read the stack trace.

Inspect logs.

Reproduce the defect.

Understand the failure.

Use AI to accelerate your investigation, but make sure you can explain the solution independently.

The objective is not to avoid AI assistance.

It is to avoid becoming dependent on an answer you cannot evaluate.

### 3. Develop architectural judgment.

Learn how to choose between different technical approaches.

Understand trade-offs involving performance, reliability, cost, security and maintainability.

AI can propose architectures and compare options.

But engineers must be able to assess whether those recommendations fit the actual constraints of a system.

### 4. Become excellent at specifying problems.

Real engineering work often begins with ambiguous requirements.

A business stakeholder describes a problem without knowing exactly what the software should do.

An engineer must translate that problem into requirements, constraints, workflows and acceptance criteria.

This capability becomes particularly valuable when multiple agents need to coordinate around a clearly defined objective.

### 5. Make verification part of the engineering process.

Generated code is not automatically production-ready code.

Understand testing, security reviews, observability, performance, compliance and deployment practices.

Ask:

Does this implementation satisfy the actual requirement?

Have the edge cases been considered?

Can the system recover from failure?

Are sensitive operations properly protected?

Can we detect problems after deployment?

The more implementation becomes automated, the more important it becomes to establish whether the resulting system can be trusted.

Here is a practical framework:

| Engineering stage | Core responsibility | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Define | Understand the problem | Are we solving the right problem? |
| Specify | Establish requirements | What does a correct solution look like? |
| Orchestrate | Coordinate AI and tools | How should the work be performed? |
| Verify | Test and inspect | Can we trust the result? |
| Own | Accept responsibility | Who is accountable when it fails? |

These activities are not permanently beyond AI's capabilities.

AI will continue expanding into more of them.

But developing expertise across the complete lifecycle is a stronger professional foundation than measuring engineering ability by the number of lines of code someone can produce.

## 9. Three Possible Futures: What Happens Next?

Nobody can reliably predict the exact structure of software engineering over the next decade.

But there are three plausible scenarios worth considering.

### Future 1: Engineers become significantly more productive.

AI handles repetitive implementation while engineers concentrate on architecture, integration, verification and product development.

Software production becomes more efficient.

The overall demand for software grows, creating opportunities for people who can use AI effectively and understand the systems they build.

### Future 2: Smaller teams produce much larger systems.

Agentic workflows allow smaller teams to develop and maintain increasingly sophisticated applications.

Experienced engineers coordinate multiple agents.

Domain experts participate directly in software creation.

The amount of software produced increases, but the number of engineers required per product decreases.

The traditional team structure changes.

### Future 3: Software production becomes increasingly commoditised.

AI becomes capable of performing larger portions of the software lifecycle.

The barrier to producing applications falls significantly.

Competition increases.

Some traditional implementation roles disappear faster than new professional responsibilities emerge.

In this scenario, the economic value of simply knowing how to translate instructions into source code could decline.

These are scenarios, not predictions.

The outcome will depend on technological capability, business adoption, market demand, regulation, education and the rate at which new tasks emerge.

Most importantly, none of these futures guarantees that the benefits will be distributed equally.

## 10. The Question Isn't Whether You Should Still Learn to Code

Let's return to Sallie Pero.

In 1915, being a computer meant something entirely different from what the word means today.

Technology transformed the occupation.

But the discipline of computation became more important.

Programming went through multiple generations of abstraction.

Compilers changed how developers wrote software.

Frameworks changed how applications were assembled.

Cloud computing changed how infrastructure was operated.

AI agents are now changing how implementation and other engineering activities are performed.

There is a common thread connecting these developments:

**Technology changes which human activities are valuable, how professions are organised and who gets access to new opportunities.**

But history also teaches us something uncomfortable.

Not everyone benefits equally.

Some tasks disappear. Some careers become less accessible. New occupations emerge, but they may require skills that displaced workers have not yet developed.

That is why I don't think the most important question is whether coding is dead.

Writing code manually will remain a meaningful skill, but it is becoming a less exclusive way to produce software.

The bigger question is whether our education systems, companies and professional communities can develop new ways for people to gain engineering experience.

We need to rethink internships, junior responsibilities, mentorship, supervised AI-assisted development and the way practical expertise is evaluated.

Companies should not simply ask how many engineers AI can replace.

They should also ask how they will train the engineers their future systems will need.

And people beginning their careers should not measure their value against how quickly they can type code compared with a machine.

They should learn how to understand problems, investigate failures, evaluate solutions and take responsibility for the systems they help create.

Because there is a difference between asking an AI to build an application and understanding whether that application deserves to exist in production.

**Perhaps coding is not disappearing. Perhaps we are witnessing the end of an era in which manually writing code was the principal way humans demonstrated their ability to build software.**

But if we automate the journey through which engineers acquire expertise, we need to build a new journey.

Otherwise, we might become very good at producing software while forgetting how to develop the people who know how to engineer it.
